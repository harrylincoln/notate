How to Convert Sheet Music to Guitar Tabs

To turn sheet music into guitar tablature, identify each note’s pitch and rhythm, then choose a string and fret that produces that pitch. A note name alone is not enough: its octave and the surrounding phrase affect where you should play it.

1. Read the notation before choosing frets

Standard notation represents pitch through a note’s vertical position on a staff. The clef establishes the reference pitches; the key signature and accidentals modify them. Note shapes, rests, ties and the time signature describe duration and timing. Read these together rather than converting only the letter names.

On a treble staff, the five lines from bottom to top are E, G, B, D, F; the spaces are F, A, C, E. Ledger lines extend the staff: middle C is on the first ledger line below a conventional treble staff. Moving to the next line or space advances one letter, cycling from G to A.

Check the key signature first. In G major, for example, F is normally sharp. In standard notation, a written accidental usually lasts for that pitch and octave until the barline; a natural cancels a sharp or flat. Notes to Tab has a limitation here: apply each out-of-key accidental separately when plotting repeated notes.

2. Understand the six tab lines

Guitar tab uses six horizontal lines for six strings. The top line is the thin, high E string (string 1); the bottom line is the thick, low E string (string 6). A number tells you which fret to press. Zero means play the open string. Read left to right; vertically aligned numbers are played together.

In standard tuning, the open strings from low to high are E2, A2, D3, G3, B3, E4. Each fret raises a pitch by one semitone. For example, fret 1 on the B string is C4 and fret 3 is D4. Basic text tab does not fully specify rhythm, so keep the original score beside it.

3. Match pitch and octave to a playable position

Guitar notation is normally written an octave above its sounding pitch. The open high E string sounds E4 but is written at the top space of a guitar treble staff, as E5. When working from piano or another concert-pitch score, preserve the sounding pitch deliberately; copying the same staff position into guitar notation would sound an octave lower.

The same pitch often occurs on several strings. Sounding E4 is available on string 1 open, string 2 fret 5, string 3 fret 9, string 4 fret 14 or string 5 fret 19. These are the same pitch, with different tone and hand positions. Choose a position that makes the next notes reachable and lets chord notes ring on separate strings.

4. Worked example: a four-note phrase

Take four quarter notes in 4/4 in C major: sounding E4, F4, G4, E4. On a conventional concert-pitch treble staff these are the bottom line, first space, second line and bottom line. In guitar notation, write them one octave higher: E5 in the top space, F5 on the top line, G5 in the space above the staff, then E5 again.

5. Use Notes to Tab for manual conversion

  1. Open the Notes to Tab converter on a computer with a mouse or trackpad. Enter the musical key and select Next Step.
  2. Select the note duration and any needed accidental. Move over the stave to preview a note, then click to place it. Each click saves the note in the current bar.
  3. Use Next and Previous to move between bars. Set minimum and maximum fret limits to constrain the available positions.
  4. Select Generate tab below. Review the output against the score and check the fingering on your guitar. The tool’s choice may differ from your preferred fingering.

This is a manual notation tool, not an automatic sheet-music reader. It does not import PDFs, photographs, audio or MusicXML. It currently works in 4/4; tightly voiced chords and advanced rhythm notation need particular care. The worked example explains pitch matching; it is not a promise that every generated fingering will match the example.

Common mistakes to check

  • Wrong octave: distinguish concert-pitch notation from guitar’s octave-transposing notation.
  • Reversed strings: the top tab line is high E, even though it is physically the lowest string in a typical playing view.
  • Missed accidentals: check the key signature, naturals and barlines. Reapply out-of-key accidentals individually in this tool.
  • Lost rhythm: fret numbers alone do not capture rests, ties, dotted notes or phrasing. Refer back to the score.
  • Unplayable chords: two simultaneous pitches cannot occupy different frets on the same string. Adjust the position or arrangement.
  • Wrong tuning: these examples assume standard E–A–D–G–B–E tuning. Alternate tunings change the mapping.
